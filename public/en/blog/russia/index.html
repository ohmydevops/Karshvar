About Karshvar

I built Karshvar to make discovering the world easier for people who love reading history. My biggest difficulty was always geography: from Anatolia to the Caucasus, from the Iranian plateau to Byzantium, I kept losing track of where important events happened.

So I started studying geography, and this game grew out of that learning. It is both a way to test myself and a step toward the offline companion I wanted for exploring the world. I plan to add more historical features over time.

Karshvar takes its name from an ancient Iranian word related to today’s Persian word for country. In Avestan cosmology, the world was described as seven regions.

Russia: Five historic places to discover

Derbent · Bolgar · Trinity Sergius Lavra · Pskov churches · Kazan Kremlin

EnglishРусский
1

Derbent

Between the Caspian Sea and the mountains, Derbent guarded a narrow passage. Its Sasanian fortifications explain that geography in stone. Follow the relationship between the citadel, the walls and the old town to understand why this location mattered.

Read the UNESCO entry
2

Bolgar

On the Volga, Bolgar preserves the memory of the Volga Bulgars and later powers. The archaeological landscape is a good place to explore cultural exchange. Look beyond individual monuments to the river setting that helped connect people and ideas.

Read the UNESCO entry
3

Trinity Sergius Lavra

This working monastery in Sergiev Posad is historic rather than ancient. Its churches and defensive features developed over the fifteenth to eighteenth centuries. Notice the contrast between the enclosing walls and the richly composed religious buildings within.

Read the UNESCO entry
4

Pskov churches

Pskov’s medieval churches offer a quieter kind of architectural discovery. Compact forms, domes, porches and belfries sit among gardens and walls. Look for these recurring details rather than expecting one enormous monumental complex.

Read the UNESCO entry
5

Kazan Kremlin

Kazan’s fortress brings several historical layers into one place. Surviving buildings largely date from the sixteenth to nineteenth centuries, with earlier remains beneath them. It is especially interesting for understanding how Tatar and Russian traditions meet.

Read the UNESCO entry
